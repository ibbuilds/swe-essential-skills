# Choosing credible validation

Use only the method needed to distinguish the live hypotheses. An experiment needs a decision it can inform, a signal that bears on that decision, a relevant population/context and an interpretation rule established before looking at the result. Thresholds are proposals unless accepted or already governed; do not fabricate statistical certainty from small or convenience samples.

## Match evidence to the question

| Uncertainty | Useful evidence | Important limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does the problem occur, and how is it handled? | Recent concrete episodes, support records, workflow observation, logs with known semantics | A complaint count lacks a denominator; an interview recollection may miss routine behavior |
| How frequent or costly is it? | Representative usage or incident data, time/cost records, sampling across relevant contexts | Instrumentation gaps, duplicate events and selection effects can distort frequency |
| Would the proposed change improve the task? | Representative prototype tasks, comparison against the current method, authorized pilot | Comprehension and task success do not establish sustained adoption or willingness to pay |
| Will people adopt or support it? | Existing switching behavior, authorized commitment or pilot, actual buyer/approver constraints | Polite enthusiasm, waitlists and hypothetical purchasing answers are weak commitments |
| Can it be delivered under constraints? | Focused engineering spike or measured dependency check | Technical feasibility is not evidence of demand or problem importance |

For interviews or observation, use recent behavior and concrete examples rather than leading participants toward the proposed solution. Include people with contrary experiences when relevant; do not choose only enthusiastic existing advocates and generalize to everyone. Distinguish separate actors' needs rather than averaging incompatible contexts into a fictional user.

For quantitative evidence, preserve the unit, denominator, time window, selection process and missingness needed to interpret the result. A change in aggregate behavior may reflect a changed population, seasonality or instrumentation rather than the intervention. Avoid causal language unless the design supports it. For causal experiments, consider assignment, contamination, exposure, guardrails and required expertise proportionate to risk.

## Plan only what is useful

Capture, briefly:

- The contested assumption and the choice it affects.
- Evidence already available and the specific gap it cannot answer.
- Proposed method, participants/data and access or consent requirements.
- Positive, negative and inconclusive interpretations, including possible confounders.
- Cost/risk boundary, stopping condition, and who can authorize or interpret the result.

Do not deploy deceptive fake-door tests, collect new personal data, contact participants or change a live product just because a research plan mentions them. Offer a lower-risk authorized alternative where possible. In high-consequence settings, establish necessary domain, ethical or regulatory review rather than treating an ordinary product experiment as adequate assurance.

After an authorized check, record actual method deviations, observed results and limitations separately from hypotheses. Negative findings can narrow the audience or disconfirm the proposed solution without disproving the underlying problem. Inconclusive findings call for a decision about the value of more evidence, not automatic expansion of the research program.
