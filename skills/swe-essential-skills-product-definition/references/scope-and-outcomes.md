# Scope, outcomes and staged choices

## Fit value to the operating market

Use the project's intended outcome and discovery evidence to connect the target
segment, its operating setting, actual alternatives and proposed benefit. A
country or demographic label alone does not identify a useful audience; establish
the situation or need that makes the value relevant. Reuse accepted targeting,
including a genuinely international or worldwide segment, rather than imposing
a local pilot. For an internal tool or library, organizational reach, workflows
and supported environments may matter more than geography.

Keep intended audience, initially supported coverage and future expansion
distinct. Evaluate only conditions that change value, adoption or viable scope:
reachable channels, language/accessibility, purchasing and payment arrangements,
connectivity/devices, integrations, support capacity or applicable jurisdiction.
Location is neither a synonym for translation nor sufficient evidence of demand.
Do not infer a legal obligation without checking its applicability.

Compare the benefit with alternatives that this audience can actually use or
would consider, including the status quo and reachable international providers.
Separate foreign analogues from direct alternatives. Evidence from another
country, segment or environment is a hypothesis for this one unless the transfer
is supported; do not turn a small local sample into a worldwide demand claim.
Research only the consequential gap or use proportionate discovery when the
opportunity itself needs investigation. Recommend feasible audience/coverage
tradeoffs with evidence and uncertainty; leave a changed product commitment to
the owner or their explicit delegation.

## Help choose value and positioning

Explain whose outcome improves, in which situation, and why this product or increment is preferable to the actual alternative. Distinguish users, purchasers, approvers and operators when their incentives differ. Positioning is a product choice about audience, problem, differentiated benefit and credible reason to believe it—not a tagline or a claim of market leadership. Where the category or comparison frame matters, recommend one that helps the intended audience understand the value without suggesting unsupported capabilities.

Help the user choose focus and explicit tradeoffs: which audience/use case comes first, which strengths matter, and what is intentionally not optimized. Tie proposed differentiators to evidence or label them hypotheses. Consider distribution/access, switching and ongoing support when they affect whether the value can be realized. For internal tools, value may be reduced risk or effort rather than revenue; for libraries, compatibility and integration costs may dominate acquisition. Do not impose SaaS funnels, subscriptions, monetization or market-size exercises on unrelated products.

Where options differ materially, compare a small set of viable directions, including improving the existing alternative or doing nothing. Use actual constraints and uncertain ranges rather than invented revenue, precision scores or a preferred technology. Recommend a direction and identify the condition that would change it. Product direction does not select a technical architecture by default.

## Define a complete slice

Trace one priority journey from its real trigger to a useful outcome. Include the minimum supporting behavior needed to make that outcome trustworthy: prerequisites, access, invalid or missing inputs, failure communication and recovery where applicable. A command-line conversion tool needs meaningful output and failure/exit behavior; it does not automatically need accounts, billing or a dashboard.

Distinguish a learning prototype from a releasable increment. A prototype may deliberately omit reliability or persistence if its use is bounded and clearly communicated. It cannot be presented as production-ready. A releasable increment still needs applicable privacy, accessibility, compatibility, operational and safety obligations even when they are not visible features. Let engineering establish the concrete contracts and verification rather than prescribing implementation details in the product brief.

When reducing scope, prefer removing whole optional use cases over damaging the main journey. State which audience, environment or failure mode is not supported, and verify that this is compatible with existing commitments. Do not make a critical capability an indefinite “later” item simply to label the first release minimal.

## Make an outcome interpretable

An outcome should name the change that matters and who experiences it. Where measurement is useful, define the population, unit, relevant time window and data source; distinguish baseline, proposed target and measured result. If the baseline is absent, propose how to establish it rather than inventing one. If measurement is not available or justified, use a qualitative acceptance method with its limitations rather than decorative metrics.

Keep outcome evidence tied to the supported audience and operating market.
An expansion needs relevant evidence or an explicit hypothesis; aggregate usage
must not hide a materially underserved segment.

Separate:

- Delivery evidence: the capability exists and passes applicable checks.
- Usage evidence: people can reach or use it in the relevant context.
- Outcome evidence: their intended task improves without unacceptable regressions.

A count of new features or total registrations is not automatically proof of value. A shorter task may still be worse if it increases errors or shifts work to another actor. Pair the intended improvement with the material countervailing effect: correctness, exclusion, burden, safety, privacy or support demand. Do not optimize a proxy against the actual user outcome.

## Prioritize under uncertainty

Avoid scoring every idea by a universal formula. Use a lightweight comparison when it changes a choice: expected user benefit, affected population, confidence, delivery and ongoing costs, downside, dependencies and reversibility. If a numeric model is useful, expose assumptions, units and sensitivity; uncertain inputs do not become precise because a spreadsheet multiplies them.

For a deadline, distinguish fixed date from desired date and mandatory scope from negotiable scope. Ask engineering to assess capacity and dependencies before claiming feasibility. Present credible alternatives—smaller audience, fewer optional paths, staged availability or a changed date—without silently trading away required quality.

For a staged plan, identify each stage's value or learning goal, prerequisite evidence, unresolved commitment and continuation/reconsideration signal. Use dates only where grounded; avoid converting uncertain distant work into artificial certainty. Pilot recruitment, telemetry changes and live rollout require their own applicable authority. A proposed continuation signal is not permission to execute the next stage automatically.

## Expert sources and limits

[April Dunford's positioning guide](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning)
connects competitive alternatives, differentiated value and best-fit customers.
Her [competition guidance](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/positioning-and-competition)
distinguishes customers' actual alternatives from merely similar products.
The [SBA market-research guide](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/market-research-competitive-analysis)
connects customer location with actual reach, demand and available alternatives.
These mechanisms inform the original procedure above; their commercial framing
is not mandatory for internal or noncommercial software. SBA's US data sources
do not establish facts or jurisdiction for another market.
